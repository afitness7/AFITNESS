1. Beginner Home Workout Guide8–10 min read

Start simple and stay consistent

You do not need an expensive gym membership to begin exercising. A little floor space, comfortable clothes and a realistic routine are enough for many beginners. Start with two or three sessions per week and leave a rest day between harder sessions.

A simple full-body routine

  • Warm up with easy marching or walking for 5 minutes.
  • Chair squats: 2 sets of 8–10 controlled repetitions.
  • Wall push-ups: 2 sets of 8–10 repetitions.
  • Glute bridges: 2 sets of 10 repetitions.
  • Bird-dog: 2 sets of 6 repetitions per side.

Finish with gentle movement and relaxed breathing. Keep the effort comfortable; you should be able to maintain good form. When the routine feels easy, add a few repetitions or another set—not everything at once.

Safety first

Stop if you feel sharp pain, dizziness or unusual shortness of breath. Choose easier variations when needed. If you have an injury, a health condition or have been inactive for a long time, ask a qualified healthcare professional what activity is suitable.

2. BMI Explained: What the Number Means6–8 min read

What is BMI?

Body Mass Index (BMI) is a screening estimate calculated from weight and height. The formula is weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. For example, if someone weighs 70 kg and is 1.75 m tall, BMI is about 22.9.

How to interpret it

For adults, commonly used categories are: below 18.5, underweight; 18.5–24.9, healthy-weight range; 25–29.9, overweight; and 30 or above, obesity range. These are screening categories, not a diagnosis. Different guidance may apply to children, teenagers, pregnancy and some populations.

What BMI cannot tell you

BMI does not directly measure body fat, muscle, fitness, fat distribution or overall health. A muscular person may have a higher BMI without having excess body fat. Use the number as one small piece of information alongside health history, waist measurement, activity and professional advice.

3. Healthy Eating for Fitness Beginners7–9 min read

Build balanced meals

Healthy eating does not require costly products or a complicated diet chart. Try to include vegetables or fruit, a protein-rich food, and a source of energy such as roti, rice, oats or other grains in your meals. Include small amounts of healthy fats too.

Affordable protein options

Useful everyday choices can include dal, chickpeas, rajma, peas, soy chunks, milk, curd, eggs, fish, chicken or paneer. Choose foods that fit your budget, preferences and dietary needs. You do not need supplements to start building healthy habits.

Simple habits that help

  • Eat a variety of foods across the week.
  • Plan meals and snacks so you are not relying only on packaged foods.
  • Eat slowly and notice hunger and fullness.
  • Avoid extreme restriction; consistency matters more than perfection.

There is no single best diet for everyone. Medical conditions, allergies, pregnancy, age and cultural preferences can change nutrition needs. Seek individual guidance from a qualified dietitian or healthcare professional when needed.

4. Daily Water Intake: Build a Hydration Habit5–7 min read

Your water needs are individual

Water supports normal body functions, temperature regulation and digestion. There is no single exact amount that suits everyone. Needs vary with body size, weather, physical activity, diet, pregnancy, breastfeeding and health conditions.

Practical ways to stay hydrated

  • Keep a water bottle nearby and sip regularly.
  • Drink more thoughtfully during hot weather and exercise.
  • Include water-rich foods such as fruits and vegetables.
  • Use thirst and urine colour as general clues, not perfect tests.

Do not force excessive water intake. Some medical conditions require fluid limits or individual instructions, so follow your clinician's advice if you have been given one.

5. Safe and Sustainable Weight-Loss Habits7–9 min read

Think long term, not quick fixes

Sustainable weight management is built through repeatable habits, not crash diets, detox products or punishing workouts. Progress can be gradual and uneven. Your energy, strength, sleep and overall wellbeing matter alongside the scale.

Five helpful habits

  • Choose balanced meals with vegetables, protein and satisfying portions.
  • Build up regular walking or another activity you enjoy.
  • Add strength exercises appropriate to your ability.
  • Prioritise sleep and a consistent daily routine.
  • Track patterns over time instead of reacting to one day's change.

Avoid very low-calorie diets, unverified pills and plans that promise dramatic results. Weight-loss advice is not appropriate for everyone, including some people who are under 18, pregnant or managing certain health conditions. Talk with a qualified healthcare professional before making major changes.
